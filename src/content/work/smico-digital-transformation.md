## The brief

SMICO is a microfinance institution operating across Central Africa, serving customers and small businesses that mainstream banks under-serve. Like many institutions of its size, it had grown faster than its operating systems. Reporting was slow, partly manual, dependent on a small number of people. Daily reconciliation across branches was a costly ritual rather than a real-time fact. AI-augmented operations were on the management agenda — but the foundations had not been built.

I was asked to produce a digital transformation strategy: what to fix, in what order, with what dependencies and what measurable outcome. The exercise was deliberate — a real strategic ask, treated as one.

## What made it hard

The hardest part of microfinance digitalization is not technology. It is the *gap between what management can articulate as a problem and what the operating reality on the ground actually is*. Reporting is slow not because the spreadsheet is bad — it is slow because the data feeding it is inconsistent across branches, because reconciliation is overnight rather than continuous, and because nobody owns end-to-end definitions of "what counts as a default" or "when does a loan get reclassified". A digital transformation that automates the existing process without naming those upstream questions automates the wrong thing faster.

The second hard part is honesty about what microfinance actually does for. Customers are sometimes the most exposed people in the financial system. Automation must not mean mechanical rejection of borderline cases that a human officer would have approved with context. The system has to encode discretion where discretion is the right answer.

## Approach

I structured the work in three sequenced workstreams, each with a measurable target.

**Operating-model first.** Before recommending any automation, I mapped the actual flow of a loan from origination through repayment to closure or default — branch by branch, role by role, the touch points and handoffs. The map made visible where definitions diverged across branches and where reporting downstream was being asked to reconcile inconsistencies that should have been resolved upstream. Recommendation: a single source of truth for customer state, with branch-level data flowing into it in near-real-time.

**Automation, where the leverage is.** Not all automation is equal. The leverage was in three places: customer onboarding (most repetitive, most paper-bound, most subject to KYC errors), portfolio reporting (hours per week of manual aggregation), and exception management (where humans were re-doing the same triage daily). I sized each opportunity in hours saved per week and risk reduced per quarter, not in vendor licenses. The roadmap is six to nine months for the first wave, with the second wave (decisioning, AI-augmented underwriting) deliberately gated on the data fabric being trustworthy first.

**Operating-model questions, by name.** A transformation document is not honest unless it names the questions it cannot answer technically. I surfaced four: governance of customer data across branches; treatment of borderline cases that should retain human review; vendor strategy and lock-in (the sector pulls hard toward foreign SaaS, with implications for sovereignty and cost); and the change-management cost of moving to a continuous-reporting culture from a monthly one. Naming these explicitly was, I think, the most useful part of the document.

## What it produced

A strategy keynote and an accompanying roadmap document, anonymized and reduced for portfolio purposes. The deliverables were structured for an executive audience but written in the kind of plain language that branch managers and operations leads could also engage with — because a strategy that only the C-suite reads is half-built.

## Reflection

The work that goes into a microfinance digital strategy is not all that different in shape from a core banking modernization. The difference is scale and stakes per decision. Microfinance customers feel automation errors more sharply because they have less margin. The ethical posture of the strategy — not over-automating discretion, naming sovereignty trade-offs in vendor selection, refusing the temptation to digitize bad processes faster — is the part that mattered most to me, and the part I would want to carry into any future engagement with similar institutions.
