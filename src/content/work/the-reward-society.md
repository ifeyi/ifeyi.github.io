## The brief

The Reward Society is a platform that lets businesses run loyalty programs and lets their customers centralize the programs they subscribe to. The product had three parts: a marketing site, an authentication middleware, and an administration console for businesses, plus a customer-facing app derived from an existing application called Optikah.

I led the design and project management. Two developers handled the build. The work had to deliver, in 15 days, a fully usable product foundation: identity, marketing site, authentication, and a re-skinned admin and customer app — under a coherent brand.

## What made it hard

The deadline was the easy part to name. The harder part was a loyalty product that was not yet sure who it was for. The original Optikah app had been built for a specific business; The Reward Society had to abstract that into a multi-tenant logic where any business could plausibly run a program. The brand had to do work the codebase was not yet doing: signal that this was *a system* rather than *one company's loyalty card*.

Project management mattered as much as design. With a 15-day window and three concurrent workstreams (vitrine, middleware, admin re-skin), the wrong handoff timing would cost a day each time. The scoping note we produced on day three of the project was the single most important document — it was what kept the team and the client aligned when individual decisions started to compound.

## Approach

**Scoping first.** The note de cadrage we wrote up front did three things: defined the product surface unambiguously (vitrine, authentication portal, admin space, customer app), named the existing Optikah codebase and the constraints inherited from it, and committed to a delivery rhythm with a checkpoint for the client at day seven. The note did not promise more than was achievable; the discipline of saying "this is the perimeter, and these are the things explicitly out of scope" was what made the rest of the work possible.

**Identity that did the abstracting work.** The wordmark and the visual system were designed around the idea of *centralization* — of bringing together the loyalty programs a customer actually has into one place. The visual language uses repetition with variation: small recurring marks that look like enrolled programs from a distance, individual identities up close. The brand does not perform "loyalty" — it performs *being a system*.

**Product UI grounded in real flows.** The administration space was designed around the actual operating tasks of a loyalty program manager: creating campaigns, tracking redemptions, understanding which customer segments respond. The screens were built so that the most common task is on the most direct path — the boring discipline of UX that often gets sacrificed to design polish.

**Daily speed-meeting cadence.** A 15-minute daily check-in for the agency team kept information moving. When something needed a longer conversation it had a longer conversation, but the daily check-in surfaced blockers before they cost a half-day of work.

## What it produced

A delivered product: marketing vitrine, authentication middleware, re-skinned customer app, updated admin console — under a coherent identity, on time. The scoping note remained a reference document for the client well after the engagement.

## Reflection

This is the project I refer back to most often when talking about how strategy and craft sit together. The temptation in a tight deadline is to skip the scoping note and the brand-thinking and just ship. That route works once. The Reward Society is the version where I did both — and the artifacts of the strategic work (the scoping note, the brand rationale) outlived the immediate engagement, in a way that let the client carry the work forward without me. That is the test of strategic design: does it survive my absence.
