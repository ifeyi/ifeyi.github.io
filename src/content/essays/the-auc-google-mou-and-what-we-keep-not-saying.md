In February 2026, the African Union Commission (AUC) and Google signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MoU) to "advance Africa's sovereign AI and digital capacity" ([AU.int press release][1]). On paper, this aligns with the AUC's [Continental AI Strategy of July 2024][2] and the broader ambition to close the gap between African states and the global technology frontier.

I want this to be a victory. I have read the strategy document carefully and I respect the people inside the AU who pushed it through. The strategy correctly identifies risks — biased data, discrimination, misinformation. Three million students will get access to advanced AI tools. Public-sector employees will work with Gemini and NotebookLM. None of that is nothing.

But there is one risk the strategy does not name, and the MoU does not address it: **digital colonialism**.

## "Sovereign" doing a lot of work

Sovereignty, in the technological sense, is not a feeling. It is a measurable property: the ability to manipulate your own data in systems you control, on infrastructure you can audit, with models you can study, modify, and replace.

By that definition, none of what the MoU establishes is sovereign. It establishes *access*. Access is good — it is necessary, even — but framing it as sovereignty collapses an important distinction.

The asymmetry matters because Google is not a charity. It is a profit-making firm operating inside a US foreign-policy framework that researchers have started calling **tech-stack diplomacy** — the practice of states promoting their domestic tech firms abroad to gain strategic advantage ([New Lines Institute][3]). The competitors here are named: China, Russia, the UAE's G42. The instrument is the stack itself. Whoever controls the layer beneath your applications controls more than your applications.

What ontology will three million African students learn? Whose definitions of "appropriate", "factual", "neutral" will get baked into the models they use? Whose business model determines which questions are answered well and which are answered quietly badly?

These are not paranoid questions. They are the same questions that drove the European Union to publish the AI Act, drove India to require data localization, and drove China to demand technology transfer as a condition of market access. We know how the answer matters because everyone else is acting as if it matters.

## What technology transfer would have looked like

The AUC strategy could have demanded what other states have routinely extracted from similar deals.

- **Source code or weights, deposited.** China requires foreign joint ventures to share meaningful technical artifacts. India has made local data residency and processing a condition of operating at scale. The EU, through the AI Act, requires significant transparency about high-risk systems. None of these are moral choices; they are bargaining outcomes.
- **Diversified partnerships.** The MoU is exclusive in spirit if not in letter — there is no parallel agreement with Hugging Face, Mistral, OpenAI, the Allen Institute, or the Chinese AI labs. A continent betting on a single foreign provider is not diversifying its dependency; it is concentrating it.
- **Compute and capacity-building tied to ownership.** Training engineers to use Gemini is not the same as training them to build models. The first creates skilled users. The second creates the basis for an ecosystem.

I know — and I want to acknowledge it openly — that the AUC negotiated from a position of structural weakness. There is no continental compute infrastructure of comparable scale. The talent base is real but distributed and underfunded. Saying "no" to Google in 2026 was not a serious option for an institution that needs results in months, not decades.

But "we cannot say no" and "we did not ask for more" are not the same sentence. The question I cannot stop asking is whether the AUC negotiating team treated this as a deal where they had little leverage and got the most they could, or as a deal where the destination — Google's reach into African infrastructure — was foreordained and the negotiation was about pace.

## What is at stake, written plainly

The futures we are imagining for our children get shaped by the systems they grow up inside. If those systems are designed three time zones away, optimized for engagement metrics determined three time zones away, and audited (when they are audited at all) under regulatory regimes designed three time zones away, then the questions our children learn to ask, and the answers they learn to expect, will be selected for them by people they will never meet.

This is the slow form of digital colonialism. It does not announce itself. It feels like progress because it *is* progress, in the narrow sense that more people get more tools. The longer-term question — who decides what the tools are for, and who profits when they are used — is a question we are agreeing not to ask.

The AUC strategy is a serious document. The MoU is a defensible compromise. Neither is sovereignty. We should call them what they are, and use the time we have to start building the things — compute, regulation, alternative partnerships, a research base — that would let us renegotiate from a different position next time.

## A closing note for technologists

If you build core banking systems in Africa, as I do, you already know what dependency on foreign vendors looks like. We have lived it for forty years. The vendors change; the structure does not. AI is the new layer, and it is being negotiated with the same playbook that gave us the previous layer.

The work of sovereignty is unglamorous: documenting our own data, building local benchmarks, training engineers who can read papers and ship systems, writing regulation that names what we want and what we will not accept. The AUC strategy is a real foundation. The MoU is a moment to be honest about how far that foundation has been built — and how much is still missing.
