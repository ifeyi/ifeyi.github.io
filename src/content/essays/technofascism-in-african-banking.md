A small import-export firm in Douala receives a wire from a Chinese counterparty. The wire passes through the global correspondent banking network, hits a US-bank compliance filter, is flagged, and quietly returned. No money moves. There is no appeal. The merchant cannot file a grievance with anyone he can name. The next time he receives a payment, he routes it through a cousin in Dubai.

The same afternoon, in the same city, the receiving bank's IT team is three months into a Temenos T24 upgrade. The contract is in dollars. The project plan was written in Geneva. Customizations the bank pays to develop will, under the master agreement, become the vendor's intellectual property.

Two events, on one afternoon, in one city. The temptation is to read them as economic events — *rent*, in Yanis Varoufakis's vocabulary. They are that. They are also more.

## Two diagnoses, layered

Varoufakis, in *Technofeudalism: What Killed Capitalism* (2023), argues that capital has been displaced by **cloud capital**: a small number of platform-owning firms that extract rent from everyone who needs to use their infrastructure to participate in the economy. The platform is the ground; everyone else is a tenant. Rent, not profit, is the dominant form of accumulation. The diagnosis lands cleanly in Africa, because the arrangement is plainly visible from here.

The frame is useful. By itself, it leaves something out, and the part it leaves out is the part that matters most for the merchant in Douala whose wire bounced.

Norman Ajari has named the missing part. The vocabulary he uses is **technofascism**: a frame for understanding contemporary digital infrastructure not only as an economic mechanism but as a political regime — one that sorts populations, manages risk, produces categories of "expendable" subject, and reproduces, through technological means, the racial logic of the colonial state.<!-- TODO: pinpoint specific Ajari source where technofascism is developed (talk, article, book) and add citation here -->

I want to walk through what the technofascism frame lets us see in African banking that the technofeudalism frame, by itself, does not — what Frédéric Lordon's analysis of desire tells us about why we keep paying — and what walking out would actually require.

## What technofascism names

The argument I take from Ajari is, in compressed form: the colonial state's project did not end with administrative independence. It mutated. Where it once produced its categories of expendable life through the plantation, the indirect rule administration, the curfew, the pass system, it now produces them through compliance regimes, risk scores, surveillance flows, and the routine algorithmic sorting that determines whose transaction clears and whose does not. The instruments are technological; the function is continuous with the colonial project that preceded them.

What is *fascist* about technofascism, in this reading, is not the aesthetic of the strong leader. It is the underlying claim of the racial state: that there exist categories of population whose lives can be foreclosed without political consequence, and that the apparatus of governance is justified — even *called for* — when it produces and manages those categories. Technofascism is what that apparatus looks like once it has migrated into platforms, payment rails, and risk engines.

This is heavier than rent. It is also more accurate.

## Four mechanisms

In African banking, four mechanisms in particular illustrate why the technofascism frame cuts deeper than the technofeudalism frame.

**The compliance regime.** Every cross-border interbank message moves through SWIFT, the Belgian cooperative that connects roughly 11,000 banks. The fees SWIFT collects are real but small. The political fact of SWIFT is large: in practice, its operations track the perimeter of the United States Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control. When OFAC sanctions a counterparty, SWIFT enforces — Iran, in 2012 and again in 2018; Russia, in 2022. African banks live continuously inside this regime — not because African states are typically the targets of sanctions, but because the *risk profile* of an African counterparty, under foreign compliance rules, is a category that produces friction by default.

This is the technofascism frame at work. The merchant whose wire was returned was not personally sanctioned. He was sorted. The category was produced upstream of him, by people who would never meet him, on the basis of a risk model that did not include him as a stakeholder. He absorbed the friction. He rerouted his life around it. He paid a cost that has no name in the rent vocabulary.

**The card-scheme regime.** When a customer pays in Lagos, Dakar, or Yaoundé with a Visa- or Mastercard-branded card, fees flow abroad. The technofeudal description is correct. What the technofascism frame adds is the *data layer*: each transaction is also a deposit into the inventory of behavioural and demographic information that the scheme operators use to model populations, set policies, allocate risk. The African card user is a participant in an economy of data they do not own, on terms they did not negotiate. The data flows out; the categorizations flow back in. Domestic switches — Nigeria's NIBSS and Verve, Morocco's CMI, GIM-UEMOA in francophone West Africa — have gained ground but have not displaced the duopoly. The data layer is what is at stake, more than the fee.

**The core banking regime.** Temenos, Infosys Finacle, Oracle FSS, TCS BaNCS, FIS, Finastra — these vendors supply the operating systems on which African banks keep their ledgers. Contracts are denominated in euros or dollars, renewed annually, structured so that customization is billable, source code is not transferred, and the upgrade cycle is dictated by the vendor's roadmap. I have written elsewhere about [the missing technology-transfer clause][1].

The technofeudal description — perpetual lease, rent in lieu of ownership — is true. The technofascism description, again, adds a layer: these systems encode an ontology — what counts as a customer, what counts as a transaction, what counts as a risk, what is logged and what is not — and the ontology was not written for African contexts. It was written for the contexts in which the vendors are headquartered and certified. African banks adapt their operations to fit the system. The fit was not free.

**The de-risking regime.** Since the post-2008 wave of US enforcement actions against European banks, the correspondent banking relationships that connect African banks to the dollar system have been thinned, repriced, and in many cases withdrawn. The Financial Stability Board has been documenting this *de-risking* trend for over a decade. The withdrawals are unilateral. They are not, in most cases, based on demonstrated misconduct. They are based on a category — *Africa* as a risk profile — that no African actor has the standing to challenge from inside the system that produces it.

This is not rent. This is a population being managed.

If we read the four mechanisms together, the technofeudal description and the technofascism description are not in competition. They are layered. The first tells us money is being extracted. The second tells us *who* is being constituted as the figure from whom extraction is unremarkable. We need both diagnoses. We have, to date, mostly used only the first.

## What rent is, on top of rent

There is a question that neither frame, by itself, answers: how does the arrangement reproduce itself, decade after decade, with the consent — and even the active enthusiasm — of the people who pay?

This is the question Frédéric Lordon spent twenty years asking about capitalism in general (*Capitalisme, désir et servitude: Marx et Spinoza*, La Fabrique, 2010). His answer, drawing on Spinoza, is that capital does not only constrain. It captures our *conatus* — our striving — by aligning what we want with what it needs. Workers are not simply coerced into the workplace. They are made to want the wages, the recognition, the career path. The arrangement reproduces because at every level it has produced subjects whose desires are already aligned with its requirements.

The same mechanism runs through African banking with rare clarity.

The bank CIO who signs the Temenos contract is not coerced. He is *choosing*, freely, between the foreign vendor with the global brand, the BIS reference clients, the gold-standard certifications — and an alternative he finds insufficient: open-source, locally maintained, less prestigious. His choice is real. His preference is real. And his preference is the *output* of a structure: the certifications, the audit firms, the conferences, the consulting networks, the training programmes, the career paths in which prestige attaches to running on the foreign platform and never to running on a fork of it.

The retail customer who carries a Visa card carries it for a real reason: it works abroad, it is accepted at every hotel, it is the card the airline expects. Her preference is real. The preference is also produced — by a network effect that the Visa scheme has been engineered for sixty years to produce.

The central bank governor who accepts the IMF's framework for de-risking and KYC accepts it because the alternative — challenging the framework — would invite consequences he, individually, cannot bear. His acceptance is rational. The rationality is the structure's, not his.

This is what Lordon's analysis lets us see: not that we are dupes — we are not — but that the arrangement does not need dupes. It needs only people optimizing for what it has made them want.

## What about mobile money?

The objection writes itself: surely mobile money is the counter-example. M-Pesa was built in Kenya, scaled across East Africa, and has done more for financial inclusion in fifteen years than fifty years of formal banking before it. Doesn't that escape the analysis?

In part, yes. M-Pesa is a genuinely African product and an operational marvel. The financial geography of East Africa has been redrawn by it. The same is true of Wave in francophone West Africa, of MTN MoMo across the continent, of OPay and Moniepoint in Nigeria.

It is also worth being precise. The cross-border layer that lets a mobile-money user in Côte d'Ivoire pay a counterparty in Senegal, in many cases, settles through intermediaries that ultimately touch SWIFT, the dollar, and the same compliance filters described above. The cloud infrastructure on which fintech rides is, in most cases, hosted by the same handful of foreign hyperscalers.

Mobile money has reorganized the *retail experience* of African banking. It has not, on its own, dismantled the four mechanisms. The platform layer has migrated; the regime underneath is largely intact, and at the cloud-hosting layer it has been deepened.

## What walking out would look like

I do not want to leave the analysis without giving a working list. So:

- **Pan-African settlement that scales.** The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS), launched by Afreximbank in collaboration with the AfCFTA Secretariat in 2022, is the first piece of infrastructure designed explicitly to settle intra-African transactions in local currencies, bypassing SWIFT and the dollar. It works. Adoption is small. The project for the next decade is to make it the default.
- **Regional card switches and instant payment rails interoperated.** NIBSS, CMI, GIM-UEMOA, and the rest are not competitors. They are pieces of the same continent. An interoperability layer with a common standard would make the card-scheme rent renegotiable.
- **An open-source core banking initiative, publicly funded.** This does not exist. It could. The economics are not the obstacle; the political will is.
- **A continental KYC and de-risking standard** — written by African regulators, adopted by African banks — that decouples participation in the regional financial system from approval in a Washington back office. This will not solve the dollar-leg problem. It will move the political centre of gravity.
- **Continental compute and AI infrastructure**, on which the next round of banking innovation can be built without immediately re-entering the same regime at a higher layer.

None of this is sufficient. All of it is necessary. The first item is operational; the others are political — and the political project is not only the dismantling of the rent. It is the dismantling of the categories that make the rent collectible from us, in particular.

## A closing note for technologists

If you build payment systems, core banking platforms, mobile money rails, or the AI layer that will sit on top of all of the above, you have already noticed that every architectural choice you make pushes either toward, or away from, the regime. Every dollar-denominated license. Every default integration to an offshore card scheme. Every compliance gateway you let route through a foreign vendor's risk engine because the procurement was easier.

These are small choices. They are also where the regime is reproduced. The vendor invoice arrives once a year. The architecture that makes the vendor invoice inevitable was decided in a sprint planning meeting, by people who had ten things on their list and chose the path of least resistance.

There is still time to choose the harder path more often. Not for the sake of a moral position — though it is also that — but because the next layer up, the AI layer, is being negotiated right now on the same playbook. If we add it on top of the four mechanisms below, we will be paying for the result, in the place I write from, for the rest of my career.

The architecture is the politics. We can build a different one.
