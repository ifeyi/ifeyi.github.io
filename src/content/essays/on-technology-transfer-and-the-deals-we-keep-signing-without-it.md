When an African bank signs a contract with a foreign core banking vendor — Temenos, Infosys Finacle, Oracle FSS, TCS BaNCS — the standard contract has no clause about source code escrow. There is no clause about training the bank's own engineers to maintain or extend the platform. There is no clause about local intellectual property in the customizations the bank pays to develop. The contract is for a license to use, priced in dollars, renewed annually, with vendor lock-in measured in decades. Decades after these systems first arrived on the continent, that is still the shape.

This is not normal. It is the specific contract African economies have kept signing — in banking, in telecoms, in mining, in agriculture — while major industrializing regions in the last century have signed a different one.

The thing they signed and we didn't is called **technology transfer**. Almost nobody on the continent treats it as a top-line negotiating demand. It is time to.

## What technology transfer actually is

Technology transfer is not "we will train your staff to use our product." That is sales. Technology transfer is the deliberate movement of know-how, intellectual property, and productive capacity from one party to another, structured so that the receiving party can eventually do without the sending party. It has a small and well-defined toolkit:

- **Mandatory joint ventures** with majority local ownership, so that foreign firms can only enter the market in partnership with a domestic firm that absorbs their know-how.
- **Source escrow and IP deposits**, so that the client gains rights to the underlying code, formula, or design under defined conditions — vendor failure, contract end, dispute.
- **Compulsory licensing** of patents, used to allow domestic manufacture of patented goods (especially pharmaceuticals) at scale and at lower cost.
- **Local content requirements** — a fixed percentage of inputs, components, or jobs must be sourced locally, and the percentage rises over time on a published schedule.
- **Data residency and localization**, requiring foreign cloud or platform operators to host and process data within the country, on infrastructure subject to local audit.
- **Tied skills transfer**: a quota of nationals trained, certified, and embedded at specific levels of the foreign firm's local operation, with publicly verifiable milestones.

None of this is moral. All of it is bargaining. Every line item is a tax on the foreign firm's freedom to operate, and the firm pays it because the local market is worth more than the cost of paying it.

## What other states extracted

The history of major industrializations in living memory is the history of states pulling technology out of foreign firms.

**Japan**, in the postwar period, built its industrial base on systematic licensing, reverse engineering, and reverse-flow training. Foreign technologies came in, were domestically reproduced, and in major sectors the domestic copies displaced foreign suppliers within a generation. MITI, the postwar industrial policy ministry, treated the negotiation of inbound technology as a national-security function, not a commercial one.<!-- TODO: if you want to cite, the canonical reference is Chalmers Johnson, *MITI and the Japanese Miracle* (1982) -->

**South Korea** built POSCO, Samsung, and Hyundai under a state-directed industrial policy that conditioned foreign technology licensing on extensive training requirements, local capacity-building, and the explicit assumption that Korean firms would eventually displace their licensors. The policy was written and enforced; the displacement was the goal.

**China**, more recently, has run the most aggressive technology-transfer regime of the modern era. For roughly thirty years, foreign companies entering the Chinese auto, energy, telecoms, and semiconductor markets were generally required to operate through joint ventures, share technical documentation, and accept that Chinese partners would eventually become independent producers. The arrangement is contested and has been formally relaxed in some sectors under WTO and US pressure, but its results are not in dispute.

**India** has used compulsory licensing of pharmaceutical patents — most famously in the 2012 Bayer *Nexavar* decision, in which the Indian Patent Office authorized Natco Pharma to manufacture a generic version of the patented cancer drug at a small fraction of Bayer's price. India's 2018 directive on the storage of payment system data has likewise forced foreign payment networks to operate domestic infrastructure under Indian regulators.

These cases differ in detail and in legitimacy. They share one feature: in each, the receiving state treated technology transfer as something it was *owed in exchange for market access*, and refused to grant access without it.

## What African states have signed instead

Now compare the African pattern across the same period.

In **banking**, a continent of more than a billion people runs on core banking software written and owned by a handful of foreign firms. There is no African open-source alternative at scale. The skills required to maintain and extend these platforms are concentrated in vendor consultancies and trained at vendor academies. Local engineers can implement; few can fork.

In **telecoms**, the GSM and now 5G stacks on which African mobile networks run have been provided for decades by Ericsson, Nokia, ZTE, and Huawei, on terms determined by them. Local operators have been encouraged to *operate* the equipment. Few have been required to *understand* it well enough to design successors.

In **extractives**, contracts negotiated through the 1990s and 2000s routinely failed to require local processing, local refining, or local engineering. The result is the export of unprocessed ore to other continents, where the value is captured. Nigeria's Oil and Gas Industry Content Development Act of 2010 is the partial exception that proves the rule — and it took an act of parliament to put it in place.

In **agriculture**, the seeds, the inputs, and increasingly the genetic data are foreign-owned and foreign-priced. A continent that feeds a tenth of humanity has remained the price-taker of its own staples.

The pattern is not random. It is an outcome of contract architecture — sustained over decades, written into laws, framed in development narratives — that systematically forecloses the demand for transfer.

## Why we have stopped extracting

I want to be honest about why. Four reasons matter, in my view.

First, **collective bargaining failure**. Africa is fifty-four states, signing fifty-four contracts. China negotiated as one buyer with 1.4 billion consumers. The African Continental Free Trade Area exists in principle and has begun to function in practice, but the institutions that would let an AU agency say "no source escrow, no contract, full stop" on behalf of all member states do not yet exist. Until they do, every state negotiates alone, and every foreign firm knows it.

Second, **donor-frame internalization**. Many of the African negotiating teams who would have to write a transfer clause were trained in institutions that frame international partnership as gift exchange — *they help us, we are grateful* — rather than as bargained extraction. The vocabulary of "partnership" and "capacity-building" makes the demand for source code feel impolite. It is not impolite. It is the question major industrializing regions have routinely put on the table without flinching.

Third, **elite alignment**. The local elites who sign these contracts are themselves often the immediate beneficiaries of the foreign relationship — through consulting fees, advisory roles, scholarships, soft postings. They are not always the agents of the national interest the contract claims to defend. This is uncomfortable to write and unavoidable to leave out.

Fourth — the one I find hardest to write — **the absence of the credible threat of walking away**. Technology transfer was extracted, in every successful case, by states that could credibly say *"if you do not concede this, we will not sign."* That credibility comes from having domestic alternatives, however inferior, that the state can fall back on while it builds. African states have rarely invested in those alternatives. The bluff has therefore not been credible. The foreign firm reads the room and offers the standard contract.

## Why AI is the rerun

The AU's [Continental AI Strategy of July 2024][1] names many risks — biased data, discrimination, misinformation. It does not, as far as I can tell, name the absence of technology transfer as one of them. The AUC–Google MoU of February 2026 — which I [have written about elsewhere][2] — extends an arrangement of *access*, not transfer.

The structure of the AI deal is the structure of the banking deal, the telecoms deal, the seed deal. Foreign firm offers product. Local user trains on product. Local user becomes dependent on product. No transfer occurs.

The difference is that AI is more concentrated than its predecessors. The number of firms in the world able to train a frontier model can be counted on two hands. The compute infrastructure required is more capital-intensive than any banking core. The window in which extraction is even *possible* — the window in which African states have any leverage at all — is narrower than the windows that opened for Japan, Korea, China, or India.

If we miss this window, the next decades' contract will look like the last decades' contract.

## What it would take

I do not want to write a piece that names the problem and gestures vaguely at solutions. So, in plain language, here is what extracting technology transfer in 2026 would require.

- An **AU-level negotiating function** for foreign technology agreements above a defined threshold. Member states would still sign; the negotiating template would be continental. The leverage compounds.
- A **standard transfer clause** for AI agreements: model weights or comparable technical artifacts deposited with a continental AI registry, retrievable on defined conditions; training-data documentation; auditable training pipelines for high-risk applications.
- **Mandatory partnerships beyond a single foreign provider.** No exclusive AI deal with any one stack. Diversification is leverage.
- **Conditional market access** for cloud and AI infrastructure: data residency, domestic operations, training quotas, local IP ownership for derived work. These are the terms India enforces, in roughly this order.
- **Investment in the credible fallback**: continental compute capacity, public model registries, open-weight infrastructure that the AU can point to when it walks away from a deal that doesn't transfer.

None of this is impossible. Most of it is administrative. The reason it has not happened is not technical or financial; it is political and habitual. We have stopped asking.

## A closing note for technologists

If you build software inside an African institution that runs on foreign core banking platforms, foreign cloud, foreign payment rails, and now foreign AI models, you already know what this contract looks like from the inside, and [the regime it produces][3]. You have read the SOWs. You have signed the NDAs. You have watched senior partners explain that the standard architecture is the standard architecture and that customization is a four-figure-an-hour conversation to be avoided.

That is the contract. It is not a fact of nature. It was a negotiated outcome, and the negotiation can be reopened.

The first move is small. It is the willingness to write the missing clause into the next agreement and watch what the vendor does. In my experience, they do not walk away. They counter. The contract that follows is a different contract.

The second move is harder, and it is collective. It is the slow construction of the alternatives that make "no" credible.

We have spent a generation building inside the contract we were given. The next is an opportunity to negotiate one we choose.
